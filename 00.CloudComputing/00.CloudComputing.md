## Introduction:

Imagine you want to start a business and need a computer server to host your website or application.

Traditional Way (Before Cloud)

You would need to:

- Buy physical servers
- Rent a data center space
- Purchase networking equipment
- Arrange power and cooling
- Hire administrators
- Handle maintenance and upgrades


This requires:

- High upfront cost
- Long setup time
- Operational complexity

If your website suddenly gets 1 million visitors, you must buy additional servers. If traffic drops later, those servers remain unused but still cost money.

This problem led to the creation of Cloud Computing.

## Essential Characteristics of Cloud Computing

1. On-Demand Self-Service - Users can create resources themselves without human intervention.
2. Broad Network Access - Users can access resources over the internet.
3. Resource Pooling - Resources are pooled together to provide flexibility and scalability.
4. Rapid Elasticity - Resources can be scaled up or down quickly to meet changing demands.
5. Measured Service - Users are billed based on their actual resource usage.


## Types of Cloud Computing

1. Public Cloud - Resources are provided by a third-party cloud provider. Layman's definition: A cloud provider that offers resources over the internet to anyone who wants to use them. Eg: AWS, Azure, GCP
2. Private Cloud - Resources are hosted on a private network. Layman's definition: A cloud provider that offers resources over the internet to anyone who wants to use them. Eg: Banks, Government Organizations, Large Enterprises
3. Hybrid Cloud - A combination of public and private cloud resources. Layman's definition: A cloud provider that offers resources over the internet to anyone who wants to use them. Eg: AWS, Azure, GCP


## Cloud Service Models


The Ultimate Layman Analogy: Pizza as a Service
===============================================

To understand the difference, imagine you want to enjoy a pizza. You have four main ways to get it,

On-Premises (Made from Scratch at Home): You buy the flour, cheese, and sauce. You use your own oven, gas, electricity, and dining table. You do everything yourself.

IaaS (Take & Bake): You go to a store and buy an uncooked pizza that someone else assembled. They provide the crust and toppings (infrastructure), but you still have to bring it home, use your own oven, and serve it at your table.

PaaS (Pizza Delivery): You order a cooked pizza. They make it, bake it, and deliver it to your door. You don't worry about the kitchen or the oven; you just provide the plates and drinks to enjoy it.

SaaS (Dining Out): You go to a pizza restaurant. They make the pizza, bake it, serve it on their plates, and clean up afterward. You just sit down, pay, and eat.


1. IaaS: Infrastructure as a Service (The "Take & Bake")

With IaaS, a cloud company rents you the absolute basics: raw computing power, empty digital storage, and networking. They take care of the heavy physical stuff (the actual computers, cooling systems, and electricity in a massive data center). However, it arrives completely blank. You are responsible for installing the operating system (like Windows or Linux), setting up security, and building your software from scratch.

    Who uses it: System administrators and IT architects.

    Real-world examples: Amazon Web Services (AWS EC2), Microsoft Azure VMs, Google Compute Engine.

    Best for: Companies that want total control over their digital environment without physically buying and maintaining hardware.


2. PaaS: Platform as a Service (The "Pizza Delivery")

PaaS gives you a step up. Instead of just giving you a blank computer, the cloud provider gives you a pre-configured platform with the operating system, database software, and development tools already installed and running.

You don’t have to worry about updating the operating system or managing the server's health. Your only job is to write your code and deploy your application.

    Who uses it: Software developers.

    Real-world examples: Heroku, Google App Engine, AWS Elastic Beanstalk.

    Best for: Developers who want to build and launch apps quickly without getting bogged down in server management.


3. SaaS: Software as a Service (The "Dining Out")

SaaS is the most common model you interact with every day. It is a fully completed software application that you access directly through your web browser or a mobile app.

You don’t build anything, you don’t manage any servers, and you don’t write code. The provider handles everything from the physical hardware up to the final user interface. You usually just pay a monthly subscription fee to use it.

    Who uses it: End-users (everyday consumers and business employees).

    Real-world examples: Netflix, Gmail, Microsoft 365, Dropbox, Slack.

    Best for: Anyone who needs a ready-made digital solution without any technical setup.


## Global Infrastructure: 

AWS Global Infrastructure is the worldwide network of physical data centers, networking equipment, and cloud services that AWS operates across the globe.

Think of AWS as a giant cloud company that owns thousands of servers distributed around the world. Instead of keeping all servers in one place, AWS spreads them across many countries and cities.

This infrastructure helps AWS provide:

- High Availability
- Fault Tolerance
- Low Latency
- Disaster Recovery
- Global Reach
- Scalability


### Main Components of AWS Global Infrastructure:

- **Regions**: The geographical area where AWS operates, such as US East, EU West, or Asia Pacific South. Minimum 3 AZs per region.

- **Availability Zones (AZs)**: The physical location within a region, such as a city or a data center.

Availability Zones (AZs)
What is an Availability Zone?

An Availability Zone is one or more data centers within a region.

Each AZ:

Has independent power
Has independent cooling
Has independent networking

Yet they are connected using ultra-fast private fiber networks.

- **Edge Locations**: The edge locations are the physical locations where AWS serves content to users. They are located near the user's location to minimize latency.

Edge Locations are small AWS facilities located closer to users.

Used mainly for:

Content Delivery
DNS Resolution
DDoS Protection

Services Using Edge Locations
=============================
Amazon CloudFront
Amazon Route 53
AWS Shield
AWS WAF


A website contains:

Images
Videos
CSS
JavaScript

CloudFront stores copies in nearby edge locations.

Users get content from the nearest location.



- **Regional Edge Caches**: The regional edge caches are the edge locations that cache content to minimize latency for users.


Edge → Regional Cache → Region

- **Local Zones**: The local zones are the edge locations that are located within a city or a data center.

Some applications need extremely low latency.

Examples:

Gaming
Video Editing
Media Rendering
Virtual Desktops



- **Wavelength Zones**: The wavelength zones are the edge locations that are located within a city or a data center.

Q1. Why Regions Exists ?

1. Reduce Latency

Users connect to the nearest region.

Example:

Tamil Nadu users usually use:

Mumbai Region (ap-south-1)

instead of

Virginia Region (us-east-1)
2. Disaster Recovery

If one region becomes unavailable, another region can be used.

Primary: Mumbai
Backup: Singapore
3. Data Residency

Some countries require data to stay within their borders.

Examples:

Government applications
Banking systems
Healthcare systems
4. Compliance Requirements

Many regulations require specific geographic storage.

Examples:

GDPR
HIPAA
PCI DSS
